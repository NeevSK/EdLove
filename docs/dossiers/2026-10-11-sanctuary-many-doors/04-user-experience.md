# UX: Familiar Doors, Honest States, and Human Continuity

## Possibility

The person chooses the expression that fits the moment: a quiet screen, short message, voice, shared school device, assistive input, or paper. The work remains recognizably theirs. They can begin through one door and inspect or correct the result through another without learning the internal architecture.

The future interface may become almost invisible. The meaning of an action, its consequence, and its recovery must remain visible. Reducing interaction count is useful only when it reduces actual effort, errors, and uncertainty.

## Actual participants, not an imagined rural persona

Study variation within roles: a teacher with a capable phone and no time; a parent with strong connectivity who prefers chat; a principal on a desktop; a family sharing a handset; a student using a screen reader; a bilingual learner whose textbook language differs from home speech; an experienced teacher who simply prefers paper. Poor signal, storage pressure, device loss, and fatigue can affect anyone.

These are research hypotheses. Observe existing workflows and ask participants which interruptions matter. Measure correction and follow-up work, not only completion clicks. Never interpret non-use as deficiency: Gurudevi may be adding no value for that task.

## Familiar channel does not mean automatic fit

WhatsApp familiarity lowers one barrier but a bot, account linking, template message, deep link, or remote voice-processing delay may introduce another. A business conversation must not be assumed to have access to existing class groups or histories. Link-only notices may exclude someone whose package covers the chat but not the browser; actual package terms need field verification.

Channel onboarding should explain the purpose and requested scope in everyday language. A parent selects which child/context a query concerns. A teacher with several classes sees the class and date before saving. Switching role changes context visibly without making the person sign up again for a separate product.

## A state vocabulary people can trust

| Actual state | Candidate plain-language presentation |
| --- | --- |
| Private local persistence | “Saved on this phone” |
| Upload waiting | “Waiting for a connection” |
| Accepted server operation | “Received; checking your record” |
| Proposal awaiting confirmation | “Draft — please check the class, date, and details” |
| Domain commit | “Saved to your classroom record” with receipt/reference |
| Notice queued | “Saved; message waiting to send” |
| Provider delivery observed | “Delivered to the selected channel” |
| Unknown external outcome | “Delivery has not been confirmed yet” |
| Conflict | “This record changed elsewhere. Review both versions” |
| Authority expired | “Your draft is safe. Sign in to check access before submitting” |

These are proposed copy examples requiring Sinhala/Tamil/English participant testing. Icons supplement words; they cannot carry state alone. Delivery does not imply reading or understanding. “Saved on this phone” does not imply recovery after device destruction.

## Confirmation without ritual friction

Display the exact effect: classroom, date, learner/count if relevant, recipient, and whether the change becomes shared. A private draft save usually needs no extra confirmation. Sharing sensitive data, recording grades, sending a notice, or executing a delegated effect needs the review appropriate to its risk.

Voice users need a short readable/listenable summary with correction. A generic Confirm button cannot identify which draft it authorizes. A revised proposal invalidates previous confirmation. When the user returns later, show the current state and expiry; do not ask them to remember conversational context.

Automation should be undoable where the effect permits it. Where reversal is impossible, say so before commitment. Repeated warning dialogs can cause users to stop reading; use consequence-specific review and avoid demanding approval for harmless navigation.

## Privacy and accessibility are interaction design

Shared-phone notifications should default to minimum disclosure and offer discreet previews. Protect Self journals from role switches and school views. Support readable font sizes, contrast, keyboard access, screen-reader labels, sufficient tap targets, script rendering, and short messages. Emoji choices remain friendly but require text labels and accessible naming.

Avoid mandatory audio: speaking may be impractical in a noisy classroom or unsafe on a shared device. Support text and manual forms. Avoid mandatory rich cards or browser jumps for every essential interaction. Preserve a clear path to human school contact for disputed or urgent matters; a bot should not impersonate an emergency service.

## Research and delivery gates

Proposed formative round: recruit a varied small set across all four Workspace expressions, with both comfortable and reluctant technology users; observe existing work before testing new flows. Run draft/correction, lost response, role switch, delayed sync, and shared-device scenarios. Record burden, comprehension, language ambiguity, privacy comfort, and support dependence. Recruitment numbers and thresholds must match access and consent; this document does not report interviews.

## Integration round R3

R2's lifecycle becomes a user-facing contract. Channel parity means consistent truth and rights, not identical layouts. Preserve rich affordances for capable users and direct essential paths for everyone. Business analysis must test whether the whole school workflow saves time; security must validate shared-device linking; product ownership must count confusion and manual recovery as delivery failures.
