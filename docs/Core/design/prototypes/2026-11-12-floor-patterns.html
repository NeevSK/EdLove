How pieces arrange on a floor

A floor never picks a layout by room. Each group of pieces answers five questions, and a small set of proven patterns follows from the answers. The same rules lay out the Classroom, Self or Library, on a phone, sideways or on a tablet.

The five questions every group answers

1. How much room is there?

Width class, and which way the screen is held. Tells us how many columns and whether panes can sit side by side.

phone · sideways · tablet · wide
2. How many pieces?

One piece is a hero. A handful can all show. Dozens need grouping, search or a shelf.

1 · 2–4 · 5–12 · 13+
3. What do people do with them?

Glance at a number, act on a task, browse similar things, or find one known thing.

glance · act · browse · find
4. How do they travel?

Stay on the floor, drill into one piece and come back, or step through one after another.

stay · drill in · step through
5. When does it matter?

Time ranks the groups. What matters right now is lit and leads; what is for later today follows; what is for anytime rests at the end. Only one thing is lit at a time.

now · later today · anytime

Who gets the screen

The floor competes with the status bar, header, perspective stripe, rooms bar and keyboard. The shell already gives way in a fixed order; this shows how much floor is left on a 360×760 phone, using what is built today.

What gives way first

  1. The keyboard and the field being typed in always win.
  2. The rooms bar slides away while reading or typing.
  3. The perspective stripe folds into the header.
  4. The header shrinks to one compact line, or leaves entirely sideways (its parts move to the rail).
  5. The action button tucks away while typing.
  6. The floor shows fewer groups: the lit one first, then by time, then by area.

What else shapes a floor

The person's comfort

Large text needs fewer columns: tiles turn into a stream. Simple mode keeps only hero and stream. Actions sit on the main hand's side.

Language

Sinhala and Tamil labels run longer than English. A tile that fits "Homework" may wrap in Sinhala, so the rule measures the label, not just the count.

Attention

Something that needs the person (marks due tomorrow, an unread notice) rises above its saved order, and settles back once seen.

Each piece's state

SanctuaryFeature decides presence. Locked and coming-soon pieces sink to the end of their group; empty groups fold to one line; offline pieces say what they still hold.

The phone's budget

On a low-end phone the verger lowers detail: artwork shelves become plain rows and nothing moves to show a peek.

Screen readers

Reading order follows the hierarchy (lit, then time, then area), not the visual columns, so a tablet board still reads in a sensible order.

Interruptions

A call or a bell mid-task: coming back restores the open piece, its scroll and any draft.

Shared phones

The arrangement belongs to the account, so a parent and child on one phone each find their own floor.

Arrangements of our own

Yes, we can have our own. Each one below is built on a proven pattern, so it keeps that pattern's strengths, but it is shaped by the world: light, paths, the day, a desk. They answer the same five questions, so the rule can pick them like any other.

Depth without moving

Most of the 3D feeling comes from placement, not animation. The eye reads depth from a handful of still cues; motion only confirms it. Turn the cues on one by one. Every cue here is placement and colour, so it costs almost nothing even on a 2 GB phone.

How it maps to us: the world's scene is the far plane, pieces are the middle plane, and the header, rooms bar and action button are the near plane. Shadows fall from where the world's sun or lamp actually is at that hour, so depth agrees with the scene. Motion adds two things on top: a piece grows into its open view, so people see where they went, and layers drift at different speeds when scrolling or tilting.

Shapes a shelf can take

A shelf does not have to be a row of rectangles. The shape should say what the thing is: people are round, books are tall, a lesson deck is a stack.

As a pattern language

Christopher Alexander described buildings with patterns that nest: town, building, room, window seat. Each pattern solves one tension and makes room for the smaller ones inside it. The floor nests the same way, and several of his patterns translate almost directly.

Sanctuary
Intimacy gradient

Public rooms at one end, private at the other: Community, Library, the work room, Self. Inside a floor, Private is the last area.

Room
Entrance transition

Entering a room is a short change of light and scene, so the person feels they arrived somewhere, not that a page loaded.

Area
Light on two sides

The lit piece (Lamp) and the world's light give every floor a clear place to look first.

Group
Alcoves

Side shelves and the desk's tray: small places beside the main space for the things kept close.

Piece
Window place

The hero: one spot with the best light where the important thing sits.

Whole
Strong centres, levels of scale

From his later work: every level has one clear centre, and sizes step down evenly (hero, card, tile, chip) instead of everything being the same size.

What I need from you

  1. Which of our own arrangements feel right (Lamp, Desk, Path, Day river, Pinboard), and is there one you already picture that is missing?
  2. Is the order of what gives way on screen right? For example, should the perspective stripe outlast the header?
  3. Which depth cues should every world use, and is turning pieces on an axis (the last cue) too much?
  4. Which shelf shapes do you want: circles for people, tall for books, stacks for decks, mixed tiles?
  5. Letting people change a group's pattern is noted for later; the layout model keeps a place for it, nothing is built.
